AI Answer — AI regulation in insurance
The idea that AI is “not really regulated” is only partly true. AI is not always regulated through one single law called “AI regulation,” especially in the United States. However, in insurance, AI is already constrained by insurance law, anti-discrimination law, data protection rules, consumer protection rules, and new AI-specific frameworks.

So the better conclusion is:

AI in insurance is not fully banned or strictly controlled, but it is increasingly regulated when it affects pricing, underwriting, claims, eligibility, or consumer treatment.

1. EU: AI in life and health insurance is now treated as high-risk
In the European Union, the EU AI Act directly affects insurance. AI systems used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance are classified as high-risk AI systems under Annex III of the AI Act. 

This does not mean insurers cannot use AI. It means they need stronger controls, such as:

risk management,

data governance,

technical documentation,

transparency,

human oversight,

accuracy and robustness requirements,

cybersecurity controls.

The European Commission explains that providers of high-risk AI systems must assess risks and design systems so that risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights are minimized. 

Interpretation
The EU is not trying to “kill” AI in insurance. It is trying to make high-impact AI systems auditable and accountable. This may slow down irresponsible use, but it probably will not stop large insurers from using AI.

2. United States: no single federal AI insurance law, but state regulation is growing
In the U.S., insurance is mainly regulated at the state level, so the system is fragmented. There is no single national AI law for insurance equivalent to the EU AI Act. However, regulators are clearly moving.

The NAIC Model Bulletin on AI use by insurers sets expectations for governance, fairness, accountability, transparency, compliance with state laws, and safe and robust AI systems. 

New York’s Department of Financial Services issued Insurance Circular Letter No. 7 in 2024, specifically addressing the use of AI systems and external consumer data in insurance underwriting and pricing. 

Colorado has gone further. Its AI law requires developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems to use reasonable care to protect consumers from algorithmic discrimination, with obligations beginning in 2026. 
 Colorado also adopted insurance-specific rules targeting algorithmic discrimination in insurance, with requirements around governance, risk management, testing, and reporting. 

Interpretation
In the U.S., regulation is not uniform, but it is clearly increasing. The main concern is not “AI exists,” but whether AI creates unfair discrimination, especially in pricing, underwriting, claims, and eligibility decisions.

3. Could regulation “break” AI growth in insurance?
Probably not. It is unlikely that regulation will completely stop AI adoption in insurance.

A more realistic outcome is:

Regulation will not break AI adoption, but it will shape which AI applications survive.

Low-risk uses will probably continue growing quickly, such as:

internal document summarization,

customer service support,

claims triage,

fraud flagging,

agent assistance,

back-office automation.

High-risk uses will face more scrutiny, such as:

pricing,

underwriting,

eligibility decisions,

claims denial,

health/life insurance risk scoring,

models using sensitive or proxy variables.

This is especially true in the EU, where life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing are explicitly classified as high-risk AI uses. 

4. Is it “too late” for regulators to interfere?
No. It is not too late.

Insurance is already a heavily regulated industry. Regulators do not need to ban AI to influence it. They can require insurers to:

document models,

test for bias,

explain decisions,

maintain human oversight,

prove compliance,

audit third-party vendors,

avoid unfair discrimination.

This matters because many AI systems in insurance affect legally sensitive outcomes: who gets coverage, how much they pay, whether a claim is accepted, and whether a customer is treated fairly.

However, your point is partly right in another sense: AI adoption is already deep enough that regulators are unlikely to simply ban it. Instead, they will probably regulate how AI is used.

5. Will regulation hurt startups more than big insurers?
Probably yes.

Large insurers usually have:

legal teams,

compliance departments,

actuarial teams,

model-risk management,

documentation processes,

money to adapt to regulation.

Smaller InsurTech startups may struggle more because compliance increases fixed costs. This could reduce experimentation and make it harder for small firms to compete.

So regulation may create a paradox:

Rules designed to control large insurers may actually strengthen them, because big companies can absorb compliance costs more easily than startups.

This does not mean regulators will avoid touching big insurers. In fact, big insurers are exactly the firms regulators care about most because they affect many consumers. But the practical burden may fall more heavily on smaller companies.

Final judgment
AI in insurance is not unregulated anymore, especially when used for pricing, underwriting, claims, or eligibility. The EU has moved toward formal high-risk AI regulation, while the U.S. is developing state-level rules and regulatory guidance.

Regulation will probably not destroy AI growth in insurance. Instead, it will separate acceptable AI from risky AI.

The strongest future AI use cases will be those that are:

explainable enough,

auditable,

compliant with anti-discrimination rules,

supported by human oversight,

useful without fully replacing human judgment.

My critical interpretation
The article from 2020 was right that AI would become important, but it did not seriously discuss regulation. With hindsight, that is a major missing point. In insurance, AI is not just a productivity tool. It can affect access to financial protection, prices, and claim outcomes. For that reason, regulation is likely to become one of the main forces shaping InsurTech after 2026.

AI Answer — Did AI change insurance market shares?
AI has clearly changed how insurance companies operate, but it has not yet radically changed who dominates the insurance market.

The insurance industry still appears to be controlled mainly by large incumbents. Smaller AI-native InsurTech companies have grown, but they have not taken enough market share to seriously displace the biggest traditional insurers.

1. Market concentration has not changed much
In U.S. property and casualty insurance, the top players remain very dominant.

In 2019, the top 10 P&C insurance groups had a cumulative market share of 47.39%. The top 25 had 65.87%. 

In 2025, the top 10 P&C insurance groups had a cumulative market share of 47.87%. The top 25 had 65.71%. 

So, from 2019 to 2025, the structure barely changed:

Year	Top 10 P&C market share	Top 25 P&C market share
2019	47.39%	65.87%
2025	47.87%	65.71%
This suggests that AI and InsurTech did not create a major redistribution of market share away from incumbents.

2. Some InsurTech firms grew, but they are still small compared to the full market
There are successful examples of technology-driven insurers.

Root, for example, became profitable in 2024. It grew policies by 21% to 414,862, and gross premiums written increased 66% to $1.3 billion. Its CEO explicitly linked this to technology and data science. 

Lemonade also shows strong operational AI adoption. In its 2024 filing, it said its claims bot handled the first notice of loss without human intervention 96% of the time, and around 55% of claims were automated. 
 In Q4 2025, Lemonade reached $1.24 billion in in-force premium and almost 3 million customers. 

However, these numbers are still small compared with the full P&C market. The U.S. P&C industry had about $1.11 trillion in direct written premiums in 2025. 

So even a fast-growing AI-native insurer with around $1–1.5 billion in premiums is still a small player at industry level.

3. AI helped small firms, but it also helped big firms
AI did not only benefit startups. Large insurers also adopted AI for:

underwriting,

claims processing,

fraud detection,

customer support,

pricing,

risk modelling,

document automation.

This matters because large insurers already have huge advantages:

more capital,

more customer data,

stronger brands,

regulatory experience,

distribution networks,

actuarial teams,

reinsurance relationships.

So AI did not automatically level the playing field. In some cases, it may even strengthen incumbents because they have the money and data needed to deploy AI at scale.

4. InsurTech changed the industry more as a technology layer than as a market-share revolution
AI-focused InsurTech firms are attracting funding. Reuters reported that in Q2 2024, global InsurTech financing rose to $1.27 billion, and around 33% of total insurance-tech funding went to AI-focused InsurTechs. The same report warned, however, that fully delegating underwriting to AI had shown limited success. 

By Q1 2026, AI-focused companies reportedly attracted 95.2% of InsurTech funding, according to Gallagher Re data cited by Insurance Business. 

This shows that investors are heavily focused on AI. But funding concentration is not the same thing as market-share disruption. Many AI InsurTechs are not replacing insurers directly. They are selling tools to insurers or helping with specific parts of the value chain.

Final judgment
AI has changed the insurance landscape, but mostly at the operational level, not yet at the market-share level.

The market still looks like this:

Big insurers remain dominant, while smaller AI-native insurers and InsurTech startups grow around them or sell technology into their operations.

A good critical conclusion would be:

AI did not destroy the traditional insurance hierarchy between 2020 and 2026. Instead, it became a tool used by both startups and incumbents. Some smaller InsurTech firms grew quickly, but the market-share data still show strong continuity. The biggest change is not that small insurers replaced big ones, but that all insurers are now under pressure to become more data-driven and automated.

Personal Comments and Critical Reflection on the Article
My first impression is that, although the article could have framed some trends more broadly, its predictions were still reasonable. With hindsight, it is easy to say that the author should have focused on broader categories such as AI in insurance operations rather than only chatbots, or IoT-based insurance rather than only wearables. However, this criticism should be treated carefully. The purpose of the article was not to perfectly predict the future of the insurance industry, but to identify concrete technologies that were becoming relevant at the time.

In this sense, I think the article made a good prediction. Chatbots, drones, and wearables were not random examples. They were specific technologies that represented a larger movement toward automation, data collection, and digital transformation in insurance. The fact that some of them later became part of broader categories does not make the article wrong. It only shows that the technologies evolved and became integrated into larger business processes.

One point that interested me in particular was the use of wearables. I have personally tried to work with wearable data in a website project, and I noticed that the issue is not only privacy. Privacy and user consent are clearly important, but there is also a technical and platform-access problem. Wearable ecosystems are often closed, and it is not always easy for external developers to access the data. For a large insurance company, this may be easier because it has more bargaining power and may be able to negotiate with companies such as Apple, Fitbit, Garmin, or other device providers. However, for smaller companies or independent developers, access to this type of data can be a major barrier.

The market-structure question also made me think. The evidence suggests that, despite technological innovation, the insurance industry is still dominated by large incumbents. This is not surprising. Insurance is a heavily regulated and risk-sensitive industry. Large insurers cannot easily experiment with unproven technologies because mistakes could harm customers, create legal problems, and generate very large financial losses. In that sense, slow adoption is not necessarily a weakness. It may actually be desirable, because insurance affects people’s lives, homes, health, and financial protection.

At the same time, this creates an interesting question: if large players remain dominant and smaller InsurTech startups rarely become major insurers, why do startups continue entering the industry? My interpretation is that startups still have incentives to innovate because they can capture value before the technology becomes fully standardized. They may also become acquisition targets, sell technology to incumbents, or specialize in parts of the insurance value chain rather than trying to become full insurance companies. Therefore, the role of startups may not always be to replace large insurers. In many cases, their role may be to test new technologies, prove that they work, and then allow larger companies to adopt them later.

This also changed how I interpret the slow transformation of insurance. At first, slow adoption may seem like a sign of inefficiency. However, after thinking about the risks, I see it more as a structural feature of the industry. Insurance companies should not adopt every new technology immediately just because it is innovative. If a chatbot, AI model, drone inspection, or wearable-data system makes wrong decisions, the consequences can be serious. For this reason, a slower and more cautious adoption process may be better for consumers and for the stability of the industry.

The chatbot trend is also interesting because I have built chatbots for clients and used them in services such as banking apps. My experience is that chatbots are useful, but only for specific tasks. They work well when the question is general and when the answer can be retrieved from a clear knowledge base. For example, a banking chatbot can explain how to send money from the app or how to activate a card feature. However, many chatbots are still not deeply personalized. If I ask whether a specific feature is active on my own account, the chatbot usually cannot answer. Technically, this information probably exists in the bank’s internal systems, but privacy, security, and regulatory concerns make this type of integration more difficult.

This suggests that the main value of chatbots is not full replacement of human workers. Instead, their value is in handling repetitive questions, guiding users through simple processes, and reducing the workload for human agents. Human support is still necessary, especially when the issue is sensitive, complex, or related to personal financial data. Even if AI becomes much more accurate, it will probably need human supervision in insurance and banking because these sectors require trust, accountability, and clear responsibility.

Overall, I think the article was directionally correct. It identified real technologies that continued to matter after 2020. However, the deeper lesson is that InsurTech transformation is not only about adopting new tools. It is about balancing innovation with risk, regulation, consumer trust, data access, and market structure. The most important insight I gained is that insurance is not slow to change only because companies are conservative. It is slow to change because the cost of technological failure can be very high.
