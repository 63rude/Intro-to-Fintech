## Article Chosen

"Three insurtech trends to watch in 2020" by Harry Menear.

## Initial AI Summary

The ChatGPT conversation summarized the article as an explanation of how InsurTech is changing insurance through:

- AI chatbots and automation.
- Drones for claim and damage assessment.
- Wearables and IoT data.

The AI summary emphasized that traditional insurance is often slow, bureaucratic, and expensive, while digital insurance tools can make service faster, cheaper, more personalized, and more data-driven.

## Explicit AI Questions Present In The Text

### Question 4

AI seems to have become one of the most important technologies in insurance. What is the current regulatory situation around AI in insurance? Is AI still mostly unregulated, or are there already specific rules? Could future regulation slow down or damage the growth of AI in InsurTech, or is the technology already too deeply adopted for regulators to seriously restrict it?

Main answer:

- AI in insurance is not fully unregulated.
- It is constrained by insurance law, anti-discrimination law, data protection rules, consumer protection rules, and AI-specific frameworks.
- EU life and health insurance risk assessment and pricing are high-risk AI uses.
- The United States has no single federal AI-insurance law, but state-level regulation and guidance are growing.
- Regulation will not destroy AI adoption, but it will shape acceptable uses.
- Smaller startups may face heavier compliance burdens than large incumbents.

### Question 5

Has AI changed the competitive landscape of the insurance industry? More specifically, did AI and recent InsurTech advances allow smaller insurance companies to gain significant market share from traditional large insurers, or does the market still look mostly dominated by the same big players as in 2020?

Main answer:

- AI changed operations more than market share.
- Large incumbents still dominate insurance.
- Smaller AI-native insurers have grown but remain small compared with the total market.
- AI helps startups, but it also helps incumbents because they already have data, capital, compliance departments, brands, and distribution.
- InsurTech may function more as a technology layer than as a direct replacement for insurers.

## Personal Comments From The Conversation

The student's reflection made these points:

- The article could have framed trends more broadly, but it was still a reasonable prediction.
- Chatbots, drones, and wearables were specific examples of broader trends: automation, data collection, and digital transformation.
- Wearable data creates privacy and consent problems, but also a practical platform-access problem because device ecosystems are closed.
- Large insurers may be better positioned than small firms to negotiate access to wearable-platform data.
- Insurance changes slowly partly because it is risky for large insurers to deploy unproven tools that could damage many customers.
- Slow adoption may actually be desirable in insurance because technological failure can affect homes, health, financial protection, and claims.
- Startups still have incentives to innovate even if they do not become dominant insurers, because they can earn money during the experimentation phase, sell tools to incumbents, specialize in parts of the value chain, or become acquisition targets.
- Chatbots are useful for general questions and repetitive processes, but they are not yet a complete replacement for humans.
- Personalized chatbot use in banking and insurance is limited by privacy, security, and regulatory concerns.
